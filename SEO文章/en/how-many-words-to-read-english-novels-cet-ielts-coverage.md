# How Many Words Do You Need to Read an English Novel? A Look at CET4 / CET6 / IELTS Coverage

Many English learners ask the same question: **"I've memorized 4,000 words — can I comfortably read an original English novel?"** The answer isn't a simple yes or no. What actually matters is the **coverage** your vocabulary gives you across the text of the book. This article uses word-frequency data to lay it all out.

## Why "Vocabulary Size" Isn't the Same as "Reading Ability"

Between vocabulary size and reading ability there's a concept you need to know: **coverage** — the percentage of words in a book that you recognize.

Say a novel has 100,000 words, and you recognize 90,000 of them. Your coverage is 90%. Research on vocabulary and reading (e.g., Nation's frequency studies) generally suggests:

- **Coverage of 95%**: you can maintain reasonably fluent reading, with a few unknown words per page.
- **Coverage of 98%**: you're at a "comfortable, effortless" level — ideal for purely recreational reading.

Here's the catch: **vocabulary growth and coverage growth are not linear.** Because the most frequent English words dominate any text, the first 2,000 words you learn can cover around 90% of a text. Every word after that adds only a fraction of a percent.

## The Frequency Distribution of Fiction

Run a **word frequency analysis** on almost any English novel and you'll see roughly this shape:

| Vocabulary (word families) | Approximate coverage of general English fiction |
|---|---|
| Most frequent 1,000 words | 75%–80% |
| 2,000 words | 88%–90% |
| 3,000 words | 92%–94% |
| 4,000 words | 95%–96% |
| 5,000 words | 96%–97% |
| 6,000 words | 97%–98% |

Notice the pattern: **going from 2,000 to 6,000 words only lifts coverage from about 90% to 98%.** That's why many people study a lot of vocabulary yet still stumble through original novels — even at 6,000 words there's still 2–3% of unknown words. On a typical page of 20–30 content words, that's one you don't know, and once you add collocations and long sentences, the reading experience suffers.

The flip side is encouraging: **once you master the most frequent core words, you already understand most of a novel.**

## Where CET4 / CET6 / IELTS Vocab Sits

Rough equivalences against common Chinese exam syllabi (approximate counts; sources vary):

- **Gaokao (China College Entrance) ≈ 3,500 words**: coverage ~93–94% — enough for the gist of children's and young-adult fiction.
- **CET-4 ≈ 4,000–4,500 words**: coverage ~95% — plenty for titles like *Charlotte's Web* or *The Little Prince*.
- **CET-6 ≈ 5,500–6,000 words**: coverage ~97% — smooth reading of most contemporary novels.
- **Chinese Postgraduate Exam ≈ 5,500 words**: same scale as CET-6, with more academic vocabulary.
- **IELTS 6.5–7.0 ≈ 6,000–8,000 words**: coverage close to 98% — toward effortless reading.

A plain rule of thumb:

> To comfortably read original English novels, aim for the **CET-6 / ~6,000-word** level. To dip your toes in, you can start with simpler books once you've passed CET-4.

## How to Know What You "Actually" Know

Memorized words aren't the same as recognized words — **"I've seen it" and "I can instantly recognize it" are very different.** Rather than counting what you've memorized, analyze an actual novel:

1. Upload the novel's `.txt` to **WordLoom**;
2. The tool counts every word and its frequency automatically;
3. Filter by **CET4 / CET6 / IELTS** tags to see exactly **how much of the book each level accounts for**;
4. Export a PDF word list with phonetics, translations, and examples, sorted from most to least frequent.

That way, you memorize the words that *actually* block you — instead of blindly studying a word list.

## Book Suggestions by Reader Level

- **Below 95% coverage (Gaokao → CET-4)**: start with children's or YA titles — *Charlotte's Web*, *The Giver*.
- **95%–97% (CET-4 → CET-6)**: contemporary popular fiction — *The Hunger Games*, the *Harry Potter* series.
- **Above 97% (CET-6+)**: classic literature, mystery, or sci-fi — *1984*, *The Kite Runner*.

## Summary

What really matters for reading an English novel is **coverage of the high-frequency words**, not the raw size of your vocabulary. Use a word-frequency analyzer to see your vocabulary distribution, start with a book at the right difficulty, read first, and build up through reading — that's the most efficient path to stronger English reading.

**If a novel is on your reading list anyway, run a frequency analysis on it first and see how smoothly you'd read it at your CET4/CET6 coverage.**
